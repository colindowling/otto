Hire Sales Engineers to Find Problems, Not Run Demos

Treating sales engineers as demo machines wastes them. Here's how to hire, brief and measure sales engineers who uncover the problems that close deals.

1 November 2026/5 min read/ANTS

When you hire a sales engineer, look for someone who finds the real problem behind a deal, not someone who runs a great demo. Hire for curiosity and discovery skill, brief them before every meeting, and measure them on technical wins, not demos delivered.

Takeaways

  • Many companies treat sales engineers as demo machines who answer tech questions.
  • The best sales engineers find the real technical problem behind the deal.
  • Hire for curiosity and discovery skill, not just product knowledge.
  • Free them from admin so their time goes to customers.

A go-to-market leader I talked with recently, who builds and runs sales engineering teams, said a lot of people look at sales engineering as a job of answering tech questions and being a demo machine. When he hires sales engineers, he tells them up front that's not what he wants.

That's the right instinct, and it changes who you hire.

The demo machine trap

It's easy to see how it happens. Account executives get a technical question they can't answer, so they pull in the sales engineer. A prospect wants to see the product, so the sales engineer runs the demo. Pretty soon, the sales engineer's calendar is wall-to-wall demos, and none of them start with a real understanding of the buyer.

The demos get polished. The win rate doesn't move.

What great sales engineers actually do

The best ones are problem finders. They:

  • Dig into how the buyer's systems work today.
  • Find where things break and what it costs.
  • Earn trust with the buyer's technical team.
  • Shape the evaluation around the problem, not the feature list.
  • Spot technical risks before they kill the deal.

The demo is the last step, tailored to what they found.

Hire for curiosity

Product knowledge can be taught. Curiosity is harder to teach. When you interview, look for:

  • Great questions. Do they ask before they tell?
  • Clear explanations. Can they explain something complex simply?
  • Honesty. Are they comfortable saying "I don't know, I'll find out"?
  • Business sense. Do they connect technical problems to money and time?

A useful interview exercise: instead of a demo, ask them to run a short discovery conversation with someone playing a buyer. You'll learn more in fifteen minutes than in an hour of slides.

Brief them before every meeting

A sales engineer walking into a meeting cold will default to a demo. Give them a short brief: who's attending, what they're trying to fix, what they've tried, what success looks like. Five minutes of prep changes the whole meeting.

Measure what matters

If you measure demos delivered, you'll get more demos. Measure instead:

  • Technical win rate.
  • How deals progress after they get involved.
  • Feedback from account executives and buyers.

Signs your sales engineers are demo machines

  • Their calendars are full of demos booked by someone else.
  • They join calls without a brief and learn the buyer's situation live.
  • Technical win rates aren't tracked at all.
  • Account executives describe them as "the demo person."

What to do this week

Pick three upcoming deals that will need a sales engineer. Before each meeting, have the account executive write a five-line brief: who's attending, what they're trying to fix, what they've tried, what success looks like, and open questions. After the meeting, ask the sales engineer what they learned that wasn't in the brief. That second answer is the value of a problem finder, and it's usually where the deal turns.

A worked example

Picture two first meetings with the same buyer. In the first, the sales engineer opens with a 40-minute demo of the platform. The buyer is polite and noncommittal. In the second, the sales engineer spends 20 minutes asking how the buyer's team handles the problem today, where it breaks and what they've already tried. Then they show only the two parts of the product that address what they heard. The second meeting is shorter, and the buyer leaves feeling understood. That's the job.

Common mistakes

  • Interviewing with a demo. You'll hire great presenters, not problem finders.
  • Measuring demos delivered. You'll get more demos.
  • Letting admin eat their week. Every hour in the CRM is an hour away from customers.

One thing to remember

The demo is the last step, not the first. Hire people who want to understand the buyer before they show anything.

Questions people ask

What does a great sales engineer do?

A great sales engineer uncovers the technical problem behind a deal, understands the buyer's environment and shows how the product solves that specific problem. They earn trust with technical stakeholders, shape the evaluation and spot risks early. Running demos and answering questions is part of the job, not the whole job.

What should I look for when hiring a sales engineer?

Curiosity, strong discovery questions, the ability to explain technical ideas simply and comfort saying 'I don't know, I'll find out.' Technical depth matters, but it can be taught on your product. Ask candidates to run a short discovery conversation in the interview rather than a demo.

How should sales engineers be measured?

On their influence on outcomes: technical win rate, deal progression after their involvement and feedback from buyers and account executives. Avoid measuring only demos delivered or questions answered, which rewards activity over impact and pushes them toward being a demo machine.

When should a sales engineer join a deal?

After an initial discovery call, once the account executive can brief them on the buyer's goals and situation. Joining earlier with no context turns the meeting into a generic demo. Joining too late means the technical buyer may have already formed an opinion.
