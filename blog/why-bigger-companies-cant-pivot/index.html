Why Bigger Companies Struggle to Absorb Outside Input and Pivot

The larger the company, the harder it is to take in new information and change course. Here's how leaders can keep a growing company able to adapt.

1 November 2026/5 min read/ANTS

Bigger companies struggle to pivot because new information has to travel through more people, layers and approvals before anyone acts, and each step adds delay and resistance. Leaders can counter it by giving outside input a named owner and a short path to a decision, and by keeping small teams with real authority to test changes.

Takeaways

  • The bigger the business, the harder it is to take in information and pivot.
  • Layers, handoffs and protected turf slow down how new information travels.
  • Give outside input a clear owner and a clear path to a decision.
  • Keep small teams with real authority to test changes.

A consultant I spoke with recently, who has advised companies of every size, said she found it frustrating that the larger the business, the more difficult it is for them to pivot, change and take in information.

Most people who've worked in both a startup and a big company have felt that difference.

Why size slows things down

It's not that people at larger companies are less smart or less willing. It's that the system works against change:

  • More layers. Information passes through several people before reaching someone who can act.
  • More approvals. Every change needs sign-off from more people.
  • Protected plans. Teams have budgets and goals built on the current direction.
  • Past success. What got you here feels safer than what might get you there.

Each one adds a little friction. Together, they can stop a good idea entirely.

The tension: stability versus adaptability

On one hand, larger companies need process. Without it, things break. On the other hand, the same process that keeps things stable makes it hard to respond when the market shifts.

I land in the middle. You need stability for running the business, and a separate, faster path for changing it.

Give outside input somewhere to go

A lot of useful information arrives from outside: customers, partners, advisors, analysts. In big companies, it often goes nowhere. Somebody hears it, mentions it in a meeting, and it fades.

Fix that with two things:

  1. A named owner. One person responsible for deciding what to do with it.
  2. A short path. A clear way for that person to test or escalate it, without waiting for the next planning cycle.

Keep small teams with real authority

Small teams move fast because they can decide. Keep a few of those inside your larger company: groups with a clear goal, a small budget and the authority to test changes without five approvals. Let them report results, not ask permission at every step.

Make it safe to be wrong

Big companies often punish failed experiments more than they reward successful ones. So nobody experiments. If you want people to bring in new information and act on it, celebrate the tests that taught you something, even when they didn't work.

Signs your company is losing the ability to pivot

  • Customer feedback gets "noted" but rarely changes anything.
  • Good ideas need three or more approvals before anyone can test them.
  • Outside advice ends up in a slide deck, not a decision.
  • People say "that's how we've always done it" without irony.

What to do this week

Think of the last piece of useful outside input your company received: from a customer, an advisor or an analyst. Trace what happened to it. Who heard it? Who decided what to do? Did anything change? If it stalled, find the step where it stopped and name an owner for that kind of input going forward.

A worked example

Say several customers tell your account managers they'd pay more for faster onboarding. In a small company, the founder hears it, tries a faster process with the next two customers and knows within a month. In a larger company, the feedback goes into a quarterly report, gets discussed at a planning meeting, waits for a budget cycle and is tested a year later, if at all. The fix isn't to drop process. It's to give that kind of input a named owner with permission to run a small test without waiting for the next planning cycle.

Common mistakes

  • Adding another committee. More review rarely speeds things up.
  • Punishing failed tests. People stop testing.
  • Assuming the feedback will find its way. It needs an owner.

One thing to remember

Size isn't the problem. Distance between information and decision is. Shorten that distance and a big company can still move fast.

Questions people ask

Why do large companies struggle to change?

More people, layers and processes mean new information has to pass through many hands before anyone acts. Teams protect existing plans and budgets. Decisions need more approvals. Past success makes people reluctant to change what worked. Each step adds delay, so by the time a decision is made, the moment may have passed.

How can a growing company stay agile?

Keep decision-making close to the people with the information. Give small teams authority to test changes. Make sure outside input, like customer feedback or expert advice, has a named owner and a path to a decision. Review assumptions regularly instead of waiting for a crisis.

How do you get leadership to act on outside advice?

Tie the advice to a specific decision and a specific owner. Present it with evidence from customers or the market, not just opinion. Propose a small test rather than a big change. Follow up with results. Advice that arrives as a clear, low-risk next step is far more likely to be acted on.

What slows down decision-making in organizations?

Unclear ownership, too many approvers, meetings without decisions, information stuck in silos and fear of being wrong. Each adds a little delay, and together they can stall good ideas for months. Fixing even one, like naming a single decision owner for each type of decision, can speed things up noticeably.
