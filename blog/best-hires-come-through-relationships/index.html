Why the Best Hires Come Through Relationships, Not Job Boards

The strongest hires and roles usually come through people who already know each other. Here's how to build a hiring network before you need it.

1 November 2026/6 min read/ANTS

The best hires and the best roles usually come through relationships, not job boards. Someone who already knows a person's work can tell you the specific story a profile never will. The catch is that you have to build those relationships long before you have an open role.

Takeaways

  • The best roles and hires often come through relationships, not job boards.
  • The specific story of why someone fits rarely shows up on a profile.
  • A hiring network has to be built long before you have an open role.
  • Relationship hiring still needs a fair, structured process once a candidate is in.

A leader I talked with recently described how he landed his current role. The CEO had met him through earlier work and later reached out to him directly in a social DM. He said he'd never put that story on LinkedIn, because it isn't the place for that kind of niche, hyper-specific story. But connecting the dots, he said, always comes through relationships.

That matches what I've seen on both sides of the table.

Why job boards miss the best fits

On the one hand, job boards are efficient. Post a role and the applications pour in.

On the other hand, a profile shows titles and dates. It doesn't show how someone handled a hard quarter, how they treat the people below them, or the strange mix of experience that makes them exactly right for this role.

Those things live in other people's memories. The only way to get at them is through someone who was there.

What a relationship tells you that a resume can't

When someone you trust says, "You need to talk to this person," you get:

  • Context. What they actually did, not just what their title was.
  • Character. How they behave when things go wrong.
  • Fit. Why this person, for this role, at this stage of your company.

That's the specific story. It's also why many of the best people never apply to anything. They get called.

Build it before you need it

The mistake most leaders make is only thinking about their network when a role opens. By then it's too late to start.

All things being equal, people will work with their friends. So the work is to stay connected to strong people even when you aren't hiring:

  1. Keep in touch with the best people you've worked with, a quick note now and then, not a pitch.
  2. Make introductions for others. Helping people is how a network stays alive.
  3. Share what your team is building in the places future hires already pay attention to.

None of it is complicated. It just takes consistency, which is the part that slips when everyone's busy.

Keep the process fair

Relationship hiring has a real risk: you end up hiring people who look and think like you.

The answer isn't to stop using your network. It's to use it to widen the pool, not skip the process. Write down what the role needs before you talk to anyone. Score every candidate on the same criteria. Use more than one interviewer. And ask your network specifically for people who are different from your current team.

A referral should get someone into the room. It shouldn't decide the outcome.

It works in both directions

This isn't only about how companies find people. It's also how good people find their next role.

The specific story of why you'd be great somewhere is hard to tell in a profile. It's easy to tell in a conversation with someone who already knows your work. So the same habits that build a hiring network also build a career: stay in touch, help others, and let people see what you're working on.

The leader I mentioned didn't land his role because of a perfect application. He landed it because the relationship was already there when the moment came. That's the whole point. You can't build it the week you need it.

A worked example

Say you need a head of operations. The job-board version gets you a few hundred applications and weeks of screening.

The relationship version starts months earlier. You've stayed in touch with a handful of strong operators from past teams. When the role opens, you send a short note to a dozen people: here's what we need and why. Within a week you have five warm introductions, each with a story about why that person fits. You still run every one through the same scorecard. But you're choosing between great people, not hunting for one.

Common mistakes

  • Only networking when you're hiring. People can tell, and it shows.
  • Letting a referral skip the interview. It feels like trust. It's actually risk.
  • Keeping the network to people like you. You'll hire the same blind spots.

What to do this week

Write down five people you'd hire tomorrow if you had the role. Send each one a short note with no ask, just to stay in touch. That's the start of a hiring network you'll be glad you have.

Questions people ask

What is the best way to find great employees?

Usually through people who already know their work. Referrals from your team, former colleagues, customers and peers give you context a resume can't. Keep in touch with strong people before you have a role, tell your network what you're looking for, and then run every candidate through the same fair, structured process.

Are referral hires better than job board hires?

Referral hires often come with more context, because someone who knows the candidate's work has already vouched for them. That can mean a better fit and a faster ramp. The risk is hiring only people like you. A clear scorecard and the same interview steps for everyone keeps referrals from becoming favoritism.

How do I build a hiring network before I need it?

Stay in touch with strong people you've worked with, even when you aren't hiring. Help others make connections. Share what your team is working on in places your future hires already pay attention to. When a role opens, you'll have people to call instead of starting from a blank job post.

How do I avoid bias in relationship-based hiring?

Use the network to widen the pool, not to skip the process. Write down what the role needs before you talk to anyone. Score every candidate against the same criteria, ask the same core questions and involve more than one interviewer. Ask your network for people unlike your current team too.
