How to Hire People Who Do More Than the Minimum

Some hires do exactly what's asked and nothing more. Here's how to spot people who do the unexpected, and how to build a team where that's normal.

1 November 2026/5 min read/ANTS

To hire people who do more than the minimum, ask for specific stories of times they went beyond what was required and dig into the details. Then build a team where initiative is possible: clear goals, room to decide how, recognition when it happens, and less busywork in the way.

Takeaways

  • Ask of every hire and every teammate: do they do the unexpected?
  • Past behavior is the best predictor, so ask for specific stories.
  • Initiative needs room: clear goals, freedom on how, and recognition.
  • Clear the busywork so people have time to go beyond it.

A leader I spoke with recently said one thing he looks for, both in hiring and in his own team, is whether people do the unexpected. Not just the job as written. Something more.

It's a simple test, and a useful one.

Why minimum effort is expensive

Someone who does exactly what's asked isn't a bad employee. But on a small team, the minimum isn't enough. There are always gaps nobody owns, problems nobody's assigned to and customers who need a bit more. People who notice and act on those are worth a lot.

People who don't leave those gaps open. And over time, gaps add up to lost customers and missed deals.

Look for patterns, not promises

Everyone says they go above and beyond in interviews. The only reliable evidence is what they've actually done. So ask for stories:

  • Tell me about something you improved that wasn't your job.
  • What's a problem you noticed before anyone asked you to?
  • What did you do in your last role that your manager didn't expect?

Then dig. What did you notice? What exactly did you do? What happened next? Real stories have details. Made-up ones get vague fast.

Look for the pattern across jobs. One story could be luck. Three is a habit.

Use a small practical test

Give candidates a short, realistic task. Not a test of skill alone, but a chance to show judgment. Do they just answer the question? Or do they notice something else, flag a risk or suggest a better approach?

You'll learn a lot from what they add that you didn't ask for.

Build the conditions for it

Even great hires stop going beyond the minimum if the environment doesn't support it. Initiative needs:

  • Clear goals. People need to know what matters before they can go further.
  • Freedom on how. Tell people what, let them figure out how.
  • Recognition. Notice it, out loud, every time.
  • No punishment. Don't reward extra effort with an extra pile of work.

Watch for it after the hire

The interview is only the start. In the first ninety days, notice whether new hires flag problems, suggest improvements or help a teammate without being asked. Tell them you noticed. Early recognition sets the tone, and it tells everyone else on the team what you value.

Signs your hiring process misses it

  • Interviews focus on skills and résumé, and never ask for stories of going further.
  • Strong hires on paper turn out to do exactly what's on the job description.
  • References describe people as "reliable" and nothing more.

What to do this week

Add one question to every interview you run: "Tell me about something you did at work that nobody asked you to do." Ask two follow-ups: why did you do it, and what happened? Write down the answers. After a few interviews you'll have a sense of what a strong answer sounds like for your team. Share that with everyone who interviews, so you're all listening for the same thing.

A worked example

Say you ask a candidate for customer success, "Tell me about something you did that nobody asked you to do." A weak answer: "I always go above and beyond for customers." A strong one: "I noticed three customers asked the same setup question in one week, so I wrote a short guide and sent it to every new account. Setup tickets dropped the next month." The second answer has a problem noticed, an action taken and a result. That's the pattern you're listening for.

Common mistakes

  • Accepting general claims. "I always go the extra mile" isn't evidence.
  • Punishing initiative with more work. People notice, and stop.

One thing to remember

The people who do the unexpected are usually the ones who notice things. Hire for noticing, and protect the time they need to act on it.

Make time for it

Questions people ask

How do you hire employees who take initiative?

Ask for specific stories of times they went beyond what was required, and dig into the details: what they noticed, what they did and what happened. Look for patterns across jobs. Give a small practical exercise and see whether they add anything nobody asked for. References can confirm the pattern.

What interview questions reveal initiative?

Try: tell me about something you improved that wasn't your job; what's a problem you noticed before anyone asked; what did you do in your last role that your manager didn't expect? Follow up with how and why. Vague answers usually mean the behavior isn't real.

Why do good employees stop going above and beyond?

Often because extra effort isn't noticed, isn't rewarded or gets punished with more work. People also stop when they're buried in busywork or unclear on what matters. Recognition, clear goals and room to decide how to do the work help keep initiative alive.

Can you teach employees to take initiative?

Partly. You can't easily teach the underlying drive, but you can create conditions that encourage it: clear goals, permission to try things, visible recognition and time freed from routine tasks. Many people show more initiative once they believe it's actually wanted.
