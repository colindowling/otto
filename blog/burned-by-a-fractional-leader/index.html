Burned by a Fractional Leader? How to Hire the Next One Right

A bad fractional hire makes companies swear off the model. Here's how to tell experienced fractional leaders apart and set the engagement up to work.

By Colin Dowling/Published 1 November 2026/Updated 1 November 2026/5 min read

To hire a fractional leader who works out, check for real operating experience at companies in your stage and situation, confirm how much of their time you'll actually get, and set clear outcomes, an internal owner and a handoff plan from day one. Most failed engagements trace back to fit and setup, not the fractional model itself.

How do you hire a fractional CRO?

Check for real operating experience at companies like yours, in your stage and situation. Confirm how much of their time you'll actually get, and set clear outcomes, an internal owner, and a handoff from day one. A title isn't enough. Most failed engagements trace back to fit and setup, not the fractional model.

Takeaways

  • Once burned by an inexperienced fractional leader, companies are slow to try again.
  • The model can be very powerful; the problem is usually the fit or the setup.
  • Check for real operating experience in your stage and situation.
  • Set clear outcomes, ownership and a handoff plan from day one.

A marketing leader I spoke with recently made a point about where the fractional market is heading. Once a company gets burned by a less experienced fractional leader, they're less willing to go back to it, even though it can be very powerful.

That's a real risk. One bad engagement can close a door that should have stayed open.

Why companies get burned

Fractional work has grown quickly, and not everyone calling themselves a fractional executive has done the job before. Common problems:

  • Not enough relevant experience. They've done the role, but at a very different company or stage.
  • Too many clients. You get a few hours a month and a lot of delay.
  • Strategy without execution. A plan gets delivered and nobody can carry it out.
  • No handoff. When the engagement ends, the capability walks out with them.

None of those are problems with the model. They're problems with fit and setup.

Check experience that matches your situation

When you interview fractional leaders, go beyond titles. Ask:

  • What companies like ours have you worked with, at our stage?
  • What specific results did you produce, and how long did it take?
  • How many clients do you carry right now?
  • How many hours a week will you actually give us?
  • Can I talk to two clients, including one where it didn't go perfectly?

Specific answers are a good sign. Vague ones are a warning.

Set it up to work

Before the engagement starts, agree on:

  1. Outcomes. What should be different in 90 days, and how you'll measure it.
  2. Time. A clear commitment in hours or days.
  3. An internal owner. Someone on your team who carries the work forward between sessions.
  4. A review point. Usually 60 to 90 days in, to decide if it's working.
  5. The handoff. What your team will be able to run on its own at the end.

Look for capability, not dependence

The best fractional leaders make themselves less necessary over time. They build systems, train your people and leave the team stronger. If six months in you can't imagine running without them, ask whether they're building your capability or their retainer.

If you got burned before

Don't write off the model. Look at what actually went wrong. Was it the person, the fit, the setup or the expectations? Fix the part that failed, and you may find the second engagement goes very differently.

Signs the engagement is going wrong

  • After 60 days, you can't point to one thing that's measurably different.
  • Your team waits for the fractional leader's next visit to make decisions.
  • Deliverables are documents, not working systems.
  • Their availability keeps shrinking.

Raise these early. A good fractional leader will want to know.

What to do this week

If you're about to hire a fractional leader, write down the three outcomes you want in 90 days before your first interview. Use them to steer every conversation: "How would you get us there, and what would you need from us?" If you're already in an engagement, ask for a 30-minute review against those outcomes. If you were burned before, write down the one thing that went wrong, and make it the first question you ask the next candidate.

A worked example

Say a company hires a fractional marketing leader for six months. In the version that fails, the leader delivers a strategy deck in month two, meets the team twice a month, and leaves. Nobody owns the plan, so it stalls. In the version that works, the same leader is paired with an internal owner from week one, sets three measurable goals for the first 90 days, and spends the last two months training that owner to run the plan alone. Same hours. Very different result.

Common mistakes

  • Hiring on title alone. Ask what they did at a company like yours.
  • No internal owner. Without one, progress stops between visits.

Questions people ask

What is a fractional executive?

A fractional executive is an experienced leader, like a CMO, CRO or CFO, who works part-time for a company, often for several companies at once. They bring senior expertise without the cost of a full-time hire. It works best for companies that need senior direction but not a full-time leader yet.

How do I choose a good fractional leader?

Look for real operating experience at companies like yours, in your stage and situation. Ask for specific results and references. Check how many clients they carry and how much time they'll give you. Ask how they hand off work to your team. A great fractional leader builds capability, not dependence.

Why do fractional engagements fail?

Common reasons include a leader without enough relevant experience, unclear goals, too little time from the leader, no one internally to carry the work forward and expectations of full-time results from part-time hours. Most failures trace back to fit and setup rather than the model itself.

How should a fractional engagement be structured?

Define the outcomes you want, the time commitment, who on your team will own the work and how progress will be reviewed. Plan for the handoff from the start: what will your team be able to run on its own when the engagement ends? Review fit after the first 60 to 90 days.
