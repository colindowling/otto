When Fast Operators Hit Stakeholder-Alignment Culture

Fast movers stall when every decision needs stakeholder alignment first. Here's how to keep speed and alignment without burning out your best operators.

By Colin Dowling/Published 1 November 2026/Updated 1 November 2026/6 min read

Fast operators stall in stakeholder-alignment cultures because every decision starts to look like it needs everyone's sign-off. The fix isn't to drop alignment. It's to save the full process for decisions that are hard to undo, give everything else a single owner, and take the follow-up busywork off people's plates.

How do you move fast when decisions need alignment?

Save the full alignment process for decisions that are hard to undo. Give everything else one owner, a short deadline, and a note to the people affected. Name one decision owner even when you do slow down. Alignment isn't the problem. Treating every decision as if it needs everyone is.

Takeaways

  • Operators who came up moving fast find stakeholder-alignment culture hard to adapt to.
  • Alignment isn't the problem. Treating every decision as if it needs full alignment is.
  • Sort decisions by how reversible they are, and only slow down the ones that are hard to undo.
  • Much of the drag is follow-up work, which is the easiest part to take off people's plates.

An operator I talked with recently described the adjustment honestly. He's adapted, he said, but it's still really hard to get him to stop and go find all the stakeholders and get alignment before moving forward.

I understand that completely. I've spent most of my career in environments where speed was the job.

The tension: speed versus buy-in

On the one hand, moving fast is how small teams win. You see a problem, you fix it, you learn something and you fix the next one.

On the other hand, as a company grows, decisions start touching more people. Skip the stakeholders and you get rework, resentment and a launch that sales didn't know was coming.

Both sides are right. The trouble starts when a company picks one answer for every decision.

Not every decision deserves the same process

Where I land: sort decisions by how hard they are to reverse.

  • Easy to undo. Changing a process, testing a message, trying a new tool for a month. Give it to one owner, set a short deadline and tell people afterward.
  • Hard to undo. Pricing, a reorganization, a big contract, a change customers will feel. These deserve the full alignment process, and fast operators should slow down for them.

Most companies run everything through the second lane. That's where the drag comes from.

Make the alignment process lighter

Even the decisions that need buy-in don't need endless meetings. Keep the process simple:

  1. Write a one-page summary: the decision, the options and a recommendation.
  2. Name one decision owner. Others give input, but one person decides.
  3. Set a date to decide. Input that arrives after the date waits for the next round.
  4. Send the decision and the reasons to everyone affected the same day.

That's alignment people can actually live with. It respects the stakeholders without making the operator wait on twelve calendars.

Where the time actually goes

When I look at what slows fast people down in these cultures, much of it isn't the decision. It's everything around it.

Chasing people for input. Reminding someone they owe a reply. Scheduling the follow-up meeting. Updating the people who missed the last one. Each task is small. Together they eat days.

That's the part that burns out good operators. They didn't sign up to be the project's reminder system.

A worked example

Say a sales leader wants to change how leads get handed from marketing to sales. In the slow version, it goes to a cross-team meeting, then another to follow up, then waits a month for everyone to weigh in. The leader spends more time chasing replies than fixing the process.

In the faster version, the team agrees it's reversible. The sales leader owns it, writes a one-page plan, gives marketing and operations three days to comment, decides and announces it. Thirty days later they review what changed. If it didn't work, they change it back.

Same people, same company. One version keeps the operator. The other slowly loses them.

What leaders owe their fast operators

If you run a team that includes people like this, the burden isn't only on them to adapt. You owe them a few things.

First, say out loud which decisions are theirs. Fast people get frustrated when the line is fuzzy, because they can't tell whether they're being careful or being slowed down for no reason.

Second, explain why the slower decisions are slower. "Because that's how we do it here" loses good people. "Because this one changes what customers pay, and we can't take it back" keeps them.

Third, protect their time. If the person who moves fastest is also the person stuck sending every reminder, you've built a system that punishes speed.

Signs your alignment culture has gone too far

  • Small, reversible changes need more than one meeting.
  • People ask "who decides?" and nobody can answer.
  • Your fastest people are the ones talking about leaving.

Common mistakes

  • Treating alignment as a vote. Input is not the same as a veto.
  • Never naming an owner. Then the decision belongs to nobody.
  • Leaving follow-up to whoever cares most. That's usually your fastest person, and it wears them down.

What to do this week

List the last five decisions that took longer than they should have. Mark each one easy or hard to undo. For every easy one, ask who could have owned it alone. That list is your new default.

Questions people ask

How do you move fast in a company that needs stakeholder alignment?

Separate decisions by how hard they are to reverse. Reversible decisions get a small owner group and a short deadline. Hard-to-reverse decisions get the full alignment process. Then make the process itself lighter: one written summary, a clear decision owner, a set date to decide and automatic follow-up afterward.

Why does stakeholder alignment slow companies down?

Usually because it's applied to everything. When every choice needs every stakeholder, meetings multiply, decisions wait on calendars and nobody knows who actually decides. The work of chasing people for input and updates also falls on the person trying to move, which drains the operators you most want to keep.

How do you keep fast-moving employees from burning out in a slow culture?

Give them clear ownership over a defined area where they can decide without a committee. Be explicit about which decisions need wider input and why. Remove the follow-up busywork that eats their time. Fast operators can live with process when they understand it and it doesn't feel like it's there for its own sake.

What is a decision owner?

A decision owner is the one person accountable for making a specific call by a specific date. Others give input, but the owner decides. Naming one keeps alignment from becoming a vote, stops decisions from drifting and tells everyone who to go to with questions once the decision is made.
