If AI Takes the Entry-Level Work, Where Do Your Future Leaders Learn?

AI is absorbing the rote work juniors used to learn on. Here's how to automate it without destroying the training ground for your next leaders.

1 November 2026/5 min read/ANTS

If AI takes the entry-level work, your future leaders learn by supervising it instead of doing it: checking AI output, handling the exceptions, and sitting closer to customers and decisions earlier. That only happens if you design it on purpose. Automate the rote work without a plan and you quietly remove the training ground.

Takeaways

  • Rote entry-level work was also where people learned how the business works.
  • Automating it without a plan removes the training ground for future leaders.
  • Move juniors from doing the rote work to checking and improving it.
  • Design learning on purpose: exposure to customers, decisions and judgment calls.

An operator I talked with recently raised a concern I keep thinking about. AI is killing entry-level and early-stage jobs that are more rote. If we're taking away the training grounds, how will we ever have senior leadership?

It's a fair question. And it's one most companies aren't asking yet.

What the rote work used to teach

My first sales job was at a semiconductor brokerage, making a lot of calls a day. It wasn't glamorous. But it taught me how buyers talk, how deals move and what the business actually depended on.

That's what rote work did for a lot of people. Data entry taught you what the data meant. Building the report taught you what leadership looked at. Answering the phones taught you what customers cared about. Nobody called it training. It was.

The tension: efficiency versus the pipeline of people

On one hand, automating rote work makes sense. It's slow, error-prone and nobody loves doing it. On the other hand, if you remove it entirely, you remove the place where people learned how the business works.

I land in the middle. Automate the work. Don't automate away the learning.

Move juniors up the work, not out of it

The approach that makes sense to me is to shift junior roles from doing the work to owning it:

  • Review the output. Someone has to check what the AI produced. That's real learning.
  • Handle the exceptions. The cases automation can't handle are where judgment is built.
  • Explain the why. Ask juniors to explain why something was done a certain way.
  • Improve the process. Let them suggest changes to how the automation works.

This keeps people close to the work, without making them do it by hand.

Design learning on purpose

When learning used to happen by accident, you didn't need a plan. Now you do. Think about what your future leaders need to understand, and build it in:

  • Time with customers early, not after three years.
  • Rotations through different functions.
  • Sitting in on decisions, with someone explaining the reasoning.
  • Real responsibilities with feedback, even small ones.

Don't stop hiring juniors

Some companies are cutting entry-level hiring altogether. It saves money this year. In five or ten years, they'll have nobody who grew up in the business. A smaller number of well-developed juniors working alongside AI is a better bet than none.

Signs you're removing the training ground

  • Junior roles have shrunk to checking boxes in a tool, with no exposure to why.
  • New hires rarely talk to a customer in their first year.
  • Your senior people can't name who would replace them in five years.
  • Hiring plans for the next year include no entry-level roles at all.

What to do this week

List the rote tasks your team has automated, or plans to, in the next year. Next to each, write one thing a junior person used to learn from doing it: how pricing works, what customers complain about, which data matters. Then decide where each lesson will be learned now. Maybe it's reviewing the AI's output weekly. Maybe it's sitting in on customer calls. If a lesson has no new home, it will quietly disappear, and you'll notice in a few years when nobody is ready to step up.

A worked example

Say a finance team automates invoice matching, a task juniors used to spend hours on. Done carelessly, the junior role becomes "click approve." Done well, the junior reviews every exception the automation flags, writes a short weekly note on the patterns they see, and joins the monthly close meeting to explain them. They're doing less typing and learning more about how the business actually runs.

Common mistakes

  • Treating review work as busywork. Checking AI output is real learning if someone explains the why.
  • Cutting every junior role at once. You can't hire ten years of experience later.

Questions people ask

Will AI replace entry-level jobs?

AI is already taking over a lot of rote work that entry-level roles used to handle, like data entry, research, first drafts and routine follow-ups. Many roles will change rather than disappear, shifting toward reviewing AI output, handling exceptions and working with customers. Companies that plan for that shift will keep a healthier talent pipeline.

How do junior employees learn if AI does the basic work?

By supervising it. Have juniors review and correct AI output, handle exceptions, sit in on customer conversations and explain why something was done a certain way. Learning comes from seeing how decisions get made and understanding the work, not just from repeating tasks by hand.

How should companies develop future leaders when automating?

Be deliberate. Map the skills people used to pick up from rote work, like how the business runs and what customers care about, and build those into roles on purpose. Pair juniors with experienced people, rotate them through functions and give them real decisions with feedback.

Should I stop hiring entry-level employees because of AI?

Be careful about that. Cutting junior hiring may save money now but leaves you without people who understand your business in five or ten years. A smaller number of well-developed juniors who work alongside AI is usually a better long-term bet than none at all.
